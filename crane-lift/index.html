Essential Mobile Crane Lift Planning Guide

Why Can't a 30-Ton Crane Lift 3 Tons at a 60-Foot Radius?

This is the most expensive mistake contractors make. A crane's manufacturer nameplate rating (e.g., "50-Ton Crane") is calculated at minimum radius—usually 8 to 10 feet from the centerpin with the boom nearly vertical. As the boom extends horizontally to reach a 60-foot setback, leverage creates massive overturning moment. At a 60-foot radius, a 50-ton hydraulic crane often has a safe chart capacity of only 6,000 to 9,000 lbs. An amateur attempting to pick 6,000 lbs with a 30-ton crane at 60 ft will trigger the crane's Load Moment Indicator (LMI) lock-out or risk structural failure.

What Outrigger Mats Are Required by OSHA?

Under OSHA 29 CFR 1926.1402, the controlling entity must ensure ground conditions are firm and adequate to support the crane. Because up to 80% of the total crane weight plus suspended load transfers onto a single corner outrigger during a swing, small steel outrigger floats will punch straight through standard asphalt or dry clay. Operators must place heavy hardwood timber cribbing mats or engineered synthetic mats under each outrigger to distribute the force over a wide enough square footage.

What Permits Are Required for Street Placement?

If any part of the crane chassis, outriggers, or counterweight swing radius encroaches on a public road or sidewalk, you must obtain a Municipal Right-of-Way / Street Obstruction Permit. Many cities require a certified Traffic Control Plan (TCP), certified flaggers, or off-duty police details. Permit processing times range from 3 to 14 business days.

When Does a Lift Become an ASME "Critical Lift"?

Under ASME B30.5 standards, a lift is classified as a "Critical Lift" when the load exceeds 75% to 85% of rated capacity on the load chart, requires tandem (multi-crane) picks, hoists hazardous materials, or hoists over occupied structures. Critical lifts mandate a formal written lift plan, rigging calculation sheets, and an on-site Lift Director.
