Essential Guide to Private Water Well Drilling

How Deep Will My Well Have to Be?

Well depth is dictated by local hydrogeology, not surface elevation. Most residential wells tap unconfined or confined aquifers between 100 and 400 feet deep. In mountainous or granite terrain, drillers often go 400 to 600+ feet to hit water-bearing fractures. Drillers check regional Department of Natural Resources (DNR) well logs from nearby parcels before arriving with the drilling rig.

Why is Submersible Pump Sizing Critical?

An oversized pump is just as destructive as an undersized one. An oversized pump pumps water faster than the aquifer recharges, sucking air or silt into the pump impeller and destroying it. An undersized pump cannot maintain 50 PSI shower pressure on the 2nd floor while irrigation runs. Correctly calculating Total Dynamic Head (TDH) matches the motor to your true water table depth.

What Are Casing and Annular Grout?

Casing is the heavy-duty pipe (PVC or carbon steel) that lines the upper portion of the borehole to prevent cave-ins. State sanitary codes require drillers to pump an impermeable bentonite clay or neat cement slurry (the "annular grout seal") into the space between the outside of the casing and the raw rock. This forms a sanitary barrier stopping surface runoff, pesticides, and septic leach field water from seeping into your drinking water.

What Happens if the Driller Hits a "Dry Hole"?

Under standard drilling contracts, property owners pay for footage drilled regardless of whether water is found. That is why working with experienced local drillers who know local geological fault lines and historic aquifer depths is critical to minimize the risk of dry boreholes.
